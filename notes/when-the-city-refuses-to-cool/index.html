Urban Climate Notes #02 · Tokyo, Japan

When the City Refuses to Cool

Tokyo, tropical nights, and the wisdom of waiting for relief

By Kenan ÇolakPublished: October 7, 2026
Urban ClimateUrban Heat IslandTokyoUrban Planning

暑さ寒さも彼岸まで。

Atsusa samusa mo higan made. “The heat and the cold last only until Higan.” [1]

Higan is the seven days centred on each equinox, tied to Buddhist observance and remembrance of ancestors [1]. The saying promises that summer’s lingering heat will ease near the autumn equinox and winter’s cold near the spring one. As a forecast it is crude. As a record of repeated experience it is hard to beat: relief tends to arrive about when the equinox does.

The proverb works on a scale of weeks. Tokyo’s problem sits on a scale of hours: relief that was expected at dusk and doesn’t come.

The night is warming faster than the day

The Japan Meteorological Agency reports a trend of about 3.5°C per century in Tokyo’s annual mean temperature for 1929–2025. The annual mean of daily maximum temperature has risen 2.2°C per century, and the daily minimum 4.6°C [2]. These are annual figures, the Tokyo series is adjusted for a station relocation [2], and they say nothing about every neighbourhood behaving alike.

They also don’t isolate the city’s share. Minimum temperatures rising faster than maximum is seen in many records around the world, so part of Tokyo’s gap is probably not urban at all. A useful comparison would be JMA’s national series, which draws on stations selected to limit urbanisation effects. I haven’t made that comparison here, and it is the one I would most like to see.

Tokyo’s annual minimum temperature has increased faster than its annual maximum temperature over the long-term record.

What the street gives back after sunset

At 8 p.m. in July the sun is gone, but the wall and the asphalt are still returning what they took in during the day. Between buildings less sky is visible, so the sky-view factor (the share of the sky hemisphere seen from a point) drops and heat escapes to space more slowly. Tight spacing slows the air, and human activity adds heat. Vegetation and moisture decide how much energy goes into evaporation instead of warming. These are the mechanisms JMA lists [3].

That is the urban heat island: the warmth of urban air relative to a less urbanised reference, typically largest on clear, calm nights . It is separate from global warming, which shifts the background. A person in a warm bedroom feels both at once, and they call for different responses: emissions policy for the background, the form and function of cities for the local difference.

After sunset, stored heat, urban geometry and human activities can slow the cooling of the urban environment.

A name for the night without relief

The Japanese term is 熱帯夜 (nettaiya), literally “tropical night”. JMA uses it for a night when the temperature stays at or above 25°C from evening to morning [4]. Thresholds vary among national meteorological services. So 25°C is a convention, not a physical line between comfort and discomfort, and rooms, bodies, humidity and air movement all change how a night feels.

JMA’s long-term analysis shows rising counts of days with a minimum of 25°C or more in several large cities. Tokyo is left out, because the effect of its station relocation on threshold counts is hard to remove [5]. I like that omission: an agency declining to publish a number it can’t defend.

A bucket of water

Uchimizu is the sprinkling of water on the ground. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government calls it Edo wisdom. It appears in Edo-period haiku and ukiyo-e, and it was also meant to settle dust, prepare an entrance for guests and purify a space [6]. The physics is evaporation, which takes heat from the wetted surface. Tokyo’s guidance recommends reused water, applied in shaded places in the morning or evening [6].

In a humid summer city the cooling is modest and short-lived, and the added moisture raises humidity. I would call uchimizu a gesture with a real physical basis, not a mitigation strategy. What I like in it is the idea that cooling a place was also a way of welcoming someone.

A cooler island inside the heat island

In August 2007, researchers working with Japan’s Ministry of the Environment measured temperatures in and around the Imperial Gardens. The monthly average inside was 1.8°C below the surrounding built-up area, and cooler air was detected up to roughly 350 m into neighbouring streets by day and about 250 m from midnight to early morning in calm conditions [7].

That is one campaign in one park, and another park will behave differently. What stays with me is the leak. On a land-use map a garden is a polygon with an edge. In the air it isn’t. For planners the question becomes where cool spaces sit, what surrounds them and how their edges connect to streets.

Traditional practices such as uchimizu and measured cooling around large green spaces illustrate two different ways in which water and vegetation can influence urban heat.

If I could add a single line to every heat-adaptation brief, it would be this: show me the 3 a.m. version. A shading scheme that works at 3 p.m. has answered half the question.
