Urban Climate Notes #04 · Phoenix, Arizona

What Temperature Are We Actually Measuring?

Phoenix, satellite heat maps, and the air above the street

By Kenan ÇolakPublished: October 21, 2026Updated: October 23, 2026

At 1:02 p.m. on 19 June 2024, NASA’s ECOSTRESS instrument on the International Space Station mapped Phoenix. Roads and adjoining sidewalks exceeded 49°C in several places, and some surfaces around Sky Harbor International Airport reached about 60°C [2]. The air temperature at the airport that day reached 43°C.

The subtraction is tempting and wrong. The 60°C reading and the 43°C daily maximum were neither simultaneous nor taken at the same spot, so 17°C is not a measured gap between surface and air. They are different quantities. ECOSTRESS recorded thermal radiation from exposed surfaces, and the airport station measured the air around its sensor. The map’s 30 m grid came from machine-learning sharpening that combined ECOSTRESS with Landsat and Sentinel-2 information [2]. The instrument's typical native footprint is about 70 by 38 metres [2].

Phoenix surface temperature and thermal burn risk on 19 June 2024 at 1:02 p.m. local time. This is a satellite-derived map of exposed surfaces, not a measurement of air temperature. Its scale, observation time and legend must remain visible in the published image. Source: NASA/JPL-Caltech, PIA25529.

What the satellite sees

A thermal sensor detects radiation emitted by the surface. Turning that into land surface temperature (LST) means correcting for the atmosphere and for the emissivity of different materials [3]. Over a city the sensor may see asphalt, a roof, bare soil or the top of a tree canopy, and the shaded pavement under that tree may not be visible at all.

Two limits are easy to forget. LST can be retrieved only where the sky is clear. And each sensor passes at its own time: Landsat in the morning, generally around 10 a.m. local solar time (the nominal equatorial crossing is about 10:12 a.m.), which is neither the afternoon peak nor the night. A hot-looking pixel on a Landsat scene is a morning surface, not the heat people feel at 4 p.m.

Air temperature comes from a thermometer shielded from the sun and exposed to the air. The surfaces around it influence it, and it still doesn’t equal them. So I try to be strict about what a heat map lets me say: it shows where exposed surfaces are warmer. An air-temperature map needs other observations, or a model built to estimate air temperature.

What recent research found

Zhang and colleagues (2026, Nature Communications) built a transfer-learning framework to estimate near-surface air temperature in 384 cities of the contiguous United States, at about 1 km resolution, for 2013–2023, around 1:30 a.m. and 1:30 p.m. local time [4]. The air temperatures are model estimates, trained and tested against available observations. There was no thermometer at every pixel.

Comparing them with satellite surface temperatures, the authors found large differences in magnitude and spatial pattern. Across the cities, surfaces tended to be warmer than the air by day and often cooler at night, and the relationship varied with local climate and position in the city. Using LST as a stand-in for air temperature could overstate heat-exposure magnitude and the differences between neighbourhoods [4]. These findings belong to that dataset, that model and those US cities. They are not a conversion factor.

For planning the implication is plain: the hottest-looking neighbourhood on a surface map may hold the hottest pavements and rooftops without having proportionally hotter air than its neighbours.

The heat a person meets

Two people wait for a bus in Phoenix at the same hour, one in full sun and one under the shelter. The air temperature at the two spots could be nearly the same while their radiation load is not. Wind, humidity and the warmth of surrounding surfaces add to the difference. The radiation part is usually summarised as mean radiant temperature.

The Heat Index combines air temperature and humidity and is intended for shaded conditions. Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) adds radiant exposure and wind. Neither is interchangeable with satellite LST [5]. If the question is a bus stop, a walking route or a schoolyard, the measurement has to match what people actually encounter. A satellite map can nominate places for closer study. It can’t replace the study.

What stays warm at night

During the July 2023 heatwave, NASA researchers used VIIRS on the Suomi NPP satellite to map Phoenix surface temperatures at about 2 a.m. on several nights. Some built surfaces were still near 38°C long after sunset, and the mapped surfaces got hotter through part of the heatwave [7]. These are night-time

surface temperatures, not air readings two metres up. They do help explain why materials and stored heat matter for the night, the theme of Note 02.

A pixel is not an observation

Landsat 8 and 9 collect thermal infrared at a native 100 m and distribute it resampled to 30 m [6]. The 30 m grid does not create independent 30 m thermal observations. The Phoenix ECOSTRESS map was sharpened with data from other satellites [2]. Cloud, emissivity, overpass time and retrieval uncertainty all add to this. None of it is a reason to give up satellite analysis. All of it is a reason to describe the analysis accurately.

Which temperature does the decision need?

My own work uses satellite LST to study how urban morphology, land-cover change and surface heating relate. Repeated imagery makes it possible to follow redevelopment and vegetation loss over large areas with very few ground sensors.

When a wooded parcel is cleared and paved, imagery can document the change and the rise in surface temperature. If the next question is warmer night air around nearby homes, that needs air-temperature evidence. If it is pedestrian exposure, it needs shade, radiation and wind. Three decisions call for three measurements.

Franklin wrote that “Half the Truth is often a great Lie” [1]. A thermal map can be accurate and still leave out the evidence a planning decision needs. Before I say anything about the people who live in a hot-looking neighbourhood, I want to know what the map showed, when, and at what real resolution.
