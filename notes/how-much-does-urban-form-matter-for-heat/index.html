Urban Climate Notes #03 · Hong Kong

How Much Does Urban Form Matter for Heat?

Hong Kong, street canyons, and the geometry of cooling

By Kenan ÇolakPublished: October 12, 2026
Urban ClimateUrban MorphologyHong KongUrban Planning

Five recent Hong Kong studies approach the question from different directions. Taken together, they make me wary of giving urban form a single temperature effect.

Same afternoon, same forecast, two streets a few blocks apart. One opens toward the sky and the prevailing wind. The other runs between closely spaced towers. Their thermal conditions need not resemble each other at all.

Calling the second street “denser” hides the differences. What matters is morphology: building height, coverage, spacing, street width and its ratio to building height, orientation, vegetation, open space, and how these combine in three dimensions. Two neighbourhoods with the same floor area ratio can have very different streets.

Shade is not the same as cooling

Shade is the most immediate effect of form. A tall building blocks direct sun and cuts the radiation load on the people below. What a pedestrian feels is heavily shaped by that radiation (summarised as mean radiant temperature) and not only by the air temperature a weather station reports . Shade also covers just one part of the energy balance. Surfaces store heat by day and give it back later, and their surroundings decide how it leaves.

The Hong Kong Observatory lists heat stored in buildings, reduced sky exposure, anthropogenic heat and lower wind speeds in dense areas among the causes of urban warming, and describes Hong Kong’s heat island as mainly a night-time phenomenon [2]. A canyon can therefore shield pedestrians at 3 p.m. while slowing longwave heat loss and ventilation at midnight. A shaded street need not be a street that cools.

The same geometry can produce useful daytime shade while affecting radiative cooling and ventilation after sunset.

Reading the sky from the street

The sky-view factor (SVF) describes part of this. In the open, much of the hemisphere above you is visible and SVF is high. Between towers the opening shrinks and SVF falls. The concept is simple. Its link to temperature isn’t.

Xu and colleagues (2024) combined satellite surface temperature with mobile air-temperature observations and tested floor area ratio, building coverage, SVF, mean height, road density and vegetation. The variables tied to daytime conditions differed from those tied to night-time ones. At neighbourhood scale, two-dimensional surface characteristics mattered most by day, while two-and three-dimensional form both mattered at night [3].

Tian and colleagues (2024) used 4,474 street-level air-temperature measurements and found the relationships mostly nonlinear. Random Forest and a neural network predicted the observations better than linear regression [4]. Hui and colleagues (2026) found SVF to be the most important morphology predictor of land surface temperature in their model, with a relationship that shifted by season and time of day and with different thresholds for January and July, day and night [5].

A caution I would add as a reader. Better prediction is a property of the model, and an “importance” ranking or a “threshold” is a statement about that model. Neither is a physical constant, and spatial autocorrelation can flatter accuracy. I read these papers as strong evidence that the association depends on context, and not as proof that no general rule exists. The physics is general: shade, storage, sky exposure, ventilation, moisture. What changes is which term dominates, at which hour, in which climate. That is also why SVF and building height stay useful as planning variables. They have to be interpreted, not applied as universal cut-offs.

Building height, street width and sky exposure describe different aspects of urban form. Similar density does not necessarily mean similar geometry.

How far does a street reach?

Zou and colleagues (2025) combined vehicle-mounted air-temperature measurements, helicopter-derived surface temperature and Google Street View imagery in Kowloon. In early-evening conditions,

the warming influence tied to surface temperature, roads and buildings extended sideways up to about 40 m, and the cooling influence of vegetation about 15 m [6].

These are properties of one city, one method and one early-evening window. Mobile traverses also require attention to sensor response and changes during the measurement period, so I wouldn’t turn the numbers into design distances. What I take from the study is that thermal influence did not stop at the parcel edge. Whether a connected green corridor does more than the same area of scattered planting is, as far as I know, still open.

From heat map to street

A satellite sees a hot surface, a station measures the air at its sensor, and a vehicle describes one route at one time. Adeniran and colleagues (2026) found that surface temperature predicted air temperature in Hong Kong, but the models improved once land use, configuration and time of day were added [7]. A heat map is not self-explanatory. Ask what it measured, at what scale and when.

What this means for planning

Street widths, spacing and height patterns outlast the plans that produced them. Hong Kong's planning guidance already addresses air ventilation assessment, and its researchers have produced urban climatic maps. What I would like to see tested is whether those tools handle the night as well as they handle the wind.

The question I would ask of any scheme is narrow: how does this configuration affect solar exposure, ventilation, heat storage and night-time cooling in the climate of this place?

There is a catch that gets little attention. Most of these studies describe today’s climate. A tower approved now will still be standing in a warmer 2060, and I have yet to see anyone show how its street will behave on a summer night then.

Urban morphology interacts with time of day, season and the temperature variable being measured. A single geometric indicator cannot describe every thermal condition.

There is an old Chinese saying, 前人種樹，後人乘涼: those before us plant the trees, and those who come after enjoy the shade. It did not originate in Hong Kong, but the Hong Kong government used it in 2018 when discussing long-term greening [1]. The trees are the easy half. The towers, the street widths and the gaps between them are inherited just as surely, by people who will live in a different climate.
