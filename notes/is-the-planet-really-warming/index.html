Urban Climate Notes #01 · Reno, Nevada

Is the Planet Really Getting Warmer?

Reno, urban climate, and the fading art of reading a place

By Kenan ÇolakPublished: October 1, 2026
Urban ClimateClimate ChangeUrban PlanningUrban Morphology

In this note

A warm year, or a warming world?Reading a placeSources

Climate Central ranks Reno first among 242 U.S. cities for warming since 1970: an estimated 7.9°F, about 4.4°C [3]. A number like that wants to be a headline. I think it deserves a second look first.

It is the slope of a straight line fitted to annual average temperatures from weather-station records for 1970–2025 [3]. A fitted slope is sensitive to where the record starts and ends, and a fifty-five-year line cannot fully separate trend from natural swings. Reno is also the top of a ranking, and the top of a ranking of 242 is partly the record that happened to run highest. None of this makes the figure wrong. It means I would quote it as an estimate for one station record, and I would like to see an uncertainty range printed beside it.

It also isn’t a measurement of Reno’s urban heat island. The city grew from 225,221 people in 2010 to an estimated 283,621 in 2025, about 26% [5]. Urban growth sits somewhere inside that temperature record, but a station series can’t say how much. Separating regional warming from urban effects takes a different analysis.

Reno, Nevada, in its regional landscape context, between the Sierra Nevada and the Great Basin.

The setting matters as much as the number. Reno lies east of the Sierra Nevada, in the mountains’ rain shadow [4], so the place had a climatic structure before anyone laid out a street. We tend to meet cities as buildings, economies and boundaries. They also sit inside a system of mountains, winds and water, and Reno is growing inside that system.

A warm year, or a warming world?

The World Meteorological Organization reports that 2015–2025 were the eleven warmest years on record, with 2025 about 1.43°C above the 1850–1900 average (±0.13°C) [6]. The IPCC concludes that human influence has unequivocally warmed the climate, and that global surface temperature has risen faster since 1970 than in any other 50-year period in at least 2,000 years [7]. A single year can still come in cooler than the one before, because natural variability keeps operating inside the trend. The trend is what counts; the year-to-year steps are noise around it.

It is tempting to set Reno’s 4.4°C beside the global 1.43°C and conclude that Reno is warming three times as fast. Don’t. The two numbers use different baselines (a 1970 trend and a 1850–1900 anomaly), cover different things (one station and the global land-and-ocean mean), and sit on different physics: land warms faster than the ocean, so almost any inland city will outrun the global figure. What the pair does show is that the global story needs a local reading.

Global warming is a long-term trend rather than the result of a single hot year.

Reading a place

I started this series from a hunch I can’t prove: that many of us have lost the habit of reading the place we live in, of knowing which street catches the afternoon wind and which courtyard keeps its heat after dark. I suspect the knowledge has gone unrecorded more than it has vanished, and that air conditioning and a forecast on the phone removed the pressure to use it. That is an impression, not a finding, and I would welcome literature on how traditional builders actually worked with climate.

Reno is a fast-growing city within a semi-arid landscape shaped by the Sierra Nevada and the Great Basin.

Benny Fillmore, a Washoe elder, speaks of a time when “we were able to understand” [1]. He is speaking about relationships with land, knowledge and stewardship around Lake Tahoe, and I don’t want to hang his words above a temperature graph as decoration. I am borrowing them for one question: have our instruments made us better readers of cities, or only better informed about them?

Residents’ sentences make a good test. “The upstairs room is unbearable in August” is no thermal assessment, but it tells you where to put a thermometer. “The water always comes back here” is no flood model either, but I would ask who remembers, for how long, and what has been built upstream since. Inherited knowledge belongs neither on a shelf nor in the bin. It should be tested.

The planet is warming. The work that interests me begins where that change reaches a particular street. Next: Tokyo, and the hours after sunset.
