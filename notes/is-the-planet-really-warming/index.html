Urban Climate Notes #01 · Reno, Nevada

Is the Planet Really Getting Warmer?

Reno, urban climate, and the fading art of reading a place

By Kenan ÇolakPublished: October 1, 2026
Urban ClimateClimate ChangeUrban PlanningUrban Morphology

In this note

“There was a time when everything … all had a voice and we were able to understand.”

Benny Fillmore, Washoe Elder [1]

I sometimes think there is an endangered species missing from our conservation lists: the city dweller who still knows how to read the place they live in.

Someone who knows which street catches the afternoon wind, which courtyard holds the heat after sunset, and where water gathers when the rain becomes serious. A tree tells them something about the soil, moisture, exposure and time.

That knowledge accumulates through living somewhere. It might survive in the memory of a flood, a planting season or a warning repeated until it becomes a proverb. I respect it because an ordinary sentence can carry years of observation.

Much of this knowledge came from necessity. Getting the season wrong could mean losing a harvest; building without understanding water could mean losing a home. Builders had to account for sunlight and weather.

Technology has made some of those dependencies less visible. We can cool a badly oriented room, import food through a failed season and consult a forecast without looking outside. These are remarkable achievements. They also make it easier to live somewhere without learning very much about it.

That is one reason I wanted to begin this series on urban climate. I want to think about what our instruments reveal alongside what people have learned by living in a place, and what we may have stopped noticing.

The biggest little city gets warmer

Reno calls itself “The Biggest Little City in the World,” a nickname displayed on its famous arch. [2] Recently, I came across the city in a temperature ranking.

Climate Central analysed annual average temperature trends for 242 U.S. cities between 1970 and 2025. Reno had the largest estimated increase: 7.9°F, approximately 4.4°C. [3] A striking number for a city with “little” in its nickname.

Reno lies east of the Sierra Nevada, in the mountains’ rain shadow, between the Sierra Nevada and the Great Basin. Its position helps determine how much moisture reaches the city and under what conditions. [4] We often describe cities through their buildings, economies or administrative boundaries. In Reno, the surrounding mountains are a reminder that a place has climatic conditions before anyone lays out a street.

Reno, Nevada, in its regional landscape context, between the Sierra Nevada and the Great Basin.

The city is growing within this semi-arid landscape. Its population rose from 225,221 in 2010 to an estimated 283,621 in 2025, an increase of approximately 26%. [5]

Reno is a fast-growing city within a semi-arid landscape shaped by the Sierra Nevada and the Great Basin.

Those population figures do not explain the temperature trend. They tell us that more people are living in a place whose recorded thermal conditions have changed substantially. The 4.4°C figure is a regression-based estimate of change in annual average temperatures from a local weather-station record. It does not measure Reno’s urban heat island. Separating regional warming from the effects of urban development requires a different analysis.

For a planner, the combination still deserves attention: Reno will need more decisions about buildings and land as both its population and its climate change.

A warm year, or a warming world?

Is the planet really warming, or are we simply passing through a warm period?

The World Meteorological Organization reports that 2015–2025 were the eleven warmest years in the observational record. In 2025, global mean near-surface temperature was approximately 1.43°C above the 1850–1900 average, with a reported uncertainty of ±0.13°C. [6]

The IPCC concludes that human activities have unequivocally caused global warming. It also assesses that global surface temperature has risen faster since 1970 than during any other 50-year period over at least the last 2,000 years. [7] Natural variability continues within that warming climate, so an individual year can still be cooler than the one before it.

Global warming is a long-term trend rather than the result of a single hot year.

Reno’s 4.4°C change since 1970 and the global anomaly of 1.43°C describe different quantities and use different reference periods. Dividing one by the other would not tell us how many times faster Reno is warming. Understanding its local trend means investigating the city and its regional climate.

Buildings still negotiate with the weather

The arrangement of buildings helps shape a street’s climate. Their height, spacing, orientation and relationship to open space influence sunlight, the exchange of radiation and the movement of air. Materials, vegetation, water and heat released by human activities also affect urban thermal conditions. [8]

A shaded street may offer relief during the day, while the same enclosing geometry can affect ventilation and the release of heat at night. Asking whether density is “good” or “bad” for climate leaves too much unanswered. Which configuration are we discussing, in which climate, at what hour, and for whom?

Local observations can help us decide what to investigate. “The upstairs room is unbearable in August” gives us something to examine, even though it is not a complete thermal assessment. If someone says, “The water always comes back here,” I would want to know who remembers, how far that memory reaches and what has since been built upstream. The observation alone cannot replace a flood model.

Inherited knowledge deserves that kind of scrutiny. Some lessons remain useful; others need reconsidering as land use and climate change. Taking them seriously includes checking where they still hold.

Learning to read again

We can observe the Earth from orbit and still know little about the thermal behaviour of the street outside our door. A satellite can help us estimate land surface temperature; a weather station can record air temperature. Neither measurement, on its own, describes the full experience of someone waiting at an unshaded bus stop. Radiation, wind and humidity matter too.

For planners, understanding a place involves choosing the right measurements and listening to the people who use it.

Benny Fillmore’s words come from a particular Washoe context of relationships with land, knowledge and stewardship around Lake Tahoe. [1] I want to keep that context in view when quoting him. His words leave me wondering how much we notice in our own cities: the climatic consequences of a building’s orientation, a lost opportunity in a sealed courtyard, or a warning in a familiar account of a flood.

We have more instruments than ever, along with accumulated experience that deserves another hearing. The planet is warming. Understanding how that change meets the places we inhabit requires us to pay closer attention to them.
